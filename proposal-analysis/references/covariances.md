# Shared systematics and covariances

## Fitting scale uncertainties

- A covariance matrix built from the measured values for a multiplicative (normalization) uncertainty biases a least-squares fit low (Peelle's pertinent puzzle; d'Agostini, Nucl. Instrum. Methods A 346, 306 (1994)). Remedies: build the covariance from the model prediction and iterate, or fit an explicit multiplicative factor per dataset with a prior of the quoted width. State the convention: does the factor multiply the data or the model, and does the prior have mean 1 or median 1? Then marginalise the factor, so that its uncertainty enters the interval, and quote the posterior mean or median rather than the joint maximum a posteriori (MAP). The joint MAP is biased at O(f²) whatever prior is chosen. With a Gaussian prior on a factor applied to the model the bias is upward, ≈ f²(1 − 1/N_sets) for comparably precise datasets (≈ +2 % for f = 20 % and two datasets in the demonstration). With a lognormal prior it is smaller and its sign depends on the true distribution of the factor (≈ −1 % in the demonstration, where the factors have mean 1). No point estimate is free of an O(f²) term under both the mean-1 and the median-1 definitions; compare it with the statistical uncertainty before worrying about it.
- Interpret fitted factors only with the convention stated: a factor below one on the data means the data lie above the model.
- Split systematics with an energy dependence (stopping powers, screening, efficiency versus E_γ) into a common scale part and a shape part, and give the shape part its own nuisance parameters acting on energy or shape. For a narrow-resonance strength, ωγ ∝ ε_r, so the stopping-power uncertainty is a pure scale; for an excitation function it has both parts.
- For low-count points use a Poisson likelihood or model-based variances; variances taken from the observed counts bias fits low as well.
- Per-dataset excess variance can absorb a shared parameter if both are free; show they are identifiable (each shared parameter constrained by at least two datasets).

## Correlations between results

- A component shared by several measurements (same laboratory, detector, efficiency reference, calibration source, stopping-power table, charge integration) correlates their results. A multiplicative shared component cancels in a ratio and an additive one in a difference. In a sum or a weighted average, a fully shared component does not average down; it adds coherently.
- Dilution: a shared component s carried by experiment i enters a rate with that experiment's weight w_i in the rate (the inverse-variance and energy-window weight), contributing w_i s to its standard deviation (≈ s/N_eff for N_eff comparably weighted experiments). Between two rates it contributes w_{A,i} w_{B,i} s_A s_B to the covariance. Treating the experiment as dominant in both overstates the covariance by ≈ N_A N_B, or by ≈ N_eff if one of the rates really is dominated by it. Compute the weights; do not assume them.
- Correlations within one compound system are handled by a joint multichannel R-matrix fit only if the shared experimental components are tied in that fit (common normalization or nuisance parameters across the segments that share them; a code that assigns one normalization per data segment, as AZURE2 does, treats the segments as independent unless a common parameter is imposed). Check this before claiming that correlation across different compound systems through shared experimental components is the new step.
- Cap a shared component at the total quoted uncertainty of each quantity it enters; never add uncertainty that is not in the published budget.

`scripts/peelle_demo.py` shows, on synthetic data with an energy-dependent model and unequal datasets, the low bias of the data-based covariance (≈ −19 % here), the absence of that bias for the iterated model-based treatment, and the residual O(f²) biases of joint-MAP factor fits with Gaussian (high) and lognormal (smaller) priors.
